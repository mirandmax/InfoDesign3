The Unequal World

How income inequality and tax revenues have evolved across continents since 1985 and what the Gini coefficient tells us about where we are today.

By Diego Grünberger, Max Mirandola and Elias Puntaier

Inequality across continents

The Gini coefficient measures the distribution of income within a country, where 0 represents perfect equality and 1 perfect inequality. Aggregated by continent, the radar chart below reveals the structural differences in how wealth is spread — differences that have proven remarkably persistent over decades.

Africa consistently records the highest levels of income inequality, while Europe's social welfare systems have kept inequality comparatively low. Asia and the Americas occupy a broad middle ground, though with considerable variation between countries.

How to read this chart: Each axis represents a continent. Values further from the center indicate higher inequality (higher Gini). The shaded area shows the mean Gini for the most recent available year.

What the bubble plot shows

Each bubble represents one country for the selected year. The x-axis shows income inequality, the y-axis shows tax revenue as a share of GDP, and bubble size reflects GDP per capita.
