The Unequal World

How income inequality and tax revenues have evolved across continents since 1985 and what the Gini coefficient tells us about where we are today.

By Diego Grünberger, Max Mirandola, Elias Puntaier

Inequality across the world today

The Gini coefficient measures the distribution of income within a country, where 0 represents perfect equality and 1 perfect inequality. This Choropleth-Map shows the Gini coefficient of each country in the year 2023.

Africa and South America consistently records the highest levels of income inequality, while Europe's social welfare systems kept inequality comparatively low. Asia and the Americas occupy a broad middle ground, though with considerable variation between countries.

An especially interesting case is India. According to its Gini coefficient, it has one of the lowest levels of income inequality in the world. However, the wealthiest 1% of the population are holding around 40% country's wealth. This shows that the Gini coefficient is not always the best measure of inequality, since it can overlook extreme wealth concentration at the top

How to read this chart: The color of the country corresponds to the Gini value of it. "Darker" countries indicate higher income inequality. "Brighter" countries indicate higher income equality.

The Intuitive Assumption

High revenue equals high redistribution. It sounds logical—but it only holds up at first glance

If we want to fight inequality, the solution seems obvious: tax more, redistribute more. If this logic holds true, countries with the highest tax revenues should boast the lowest levels of inequality. At first glance, the data seems to back this up: Western and Northern Europe (high taxes) cluster tightly in the low-inequality corner. Parts of Africa and Latin America stand in stark contrast on the opposite side.

But appearances can be deceiving. Watch what happens when we scale the circles by economic power (GDP per capita) and color them by continent. Suddenly, the illusion fades. The data points scatter wildly. Countries with identical tax ratios exhibit vastly different Gini coefficients. The hidden driver behind this beautiful correlation isn't the tax rate itself—it's wealth. Rich nations possess deep-rooted, stable institutions; they can afford to collect high taxes because they already have a robust middle class.

Higher taxes are often a symptom of a wealthy economy, not a magic cure for economic disparity. If taxation were the actual engine of equality, then a country increasing its tax revenue over time should naturally see its Gini curve bend downward. Does it? Let’s push the timeline forward and see...

The Test Over Time

Up to this point, the intuitive explanation seems convincing: countries that collect more tax revenue should be better at reducing inequality. But once the time dimension is introduced, that relationship becomes less convincing.

The small multiples show two parallel developments for each country: prosperity (GDP per capita) and inequality (Gini) over more than three decades. What emerges is that rising incomes and growing government revenues do not automatically translate into a more equal society.

Some countries become significantly wealthier while inequality barely changes. Others achieve meaningful reductions in inequality despite collecting comparatively less revenue. The difference is not simply how much money enters the state — but how that money is used.

The perspective therefore shifts: taxes create capacity, but they do not create equality on their own.

The real question is no longer: How high are taxes?
Instead, it becomes: What are those revenues spent on?

Conclusion — “Taxes Alone Are Not Enough”

Together, the three visualizations tell a different story than the intuitive assumption.

The map shows where inequality is most severe. The scatter plot suggests a relationship with taxation — but also reveals major differences between countries with similar tax levels. The time series ultimately shows why that relationship remains incomplete.

Tax revenue creates room for action. Whether that leads to lower inequality depends on political choices and public spending.

That is why tax levels alone cannot explain why some societies are more equal than others.

The key question is not: How much does the government collect? But: How does it distribute what it collects?
